## What is this?

A sensor loses sight of a moving object from 3 to 6 seconds. The upper panel shows the [Kalman filter](/pieces/kalman-filtering) carrying its last estimate through the gap. With no orange measurements to correct it, the dashed navy line drifts from the grey true path and its uncertainty band widens. When the sensor returns, the filter corrects its current estimate. The estimates it recorded inside the gap stay where they were.

The lower panel uses the same measurements, including the ones after the gap, to estimate where the object was. Its solid navy line is the smoothed trajectory. Later positions constrain earlier positions and velocities through the motion model, pulling the reconstruction towards the far end of the gap and narrowing the band inside it.

The measurement cutoff controls how much of the record is available. Set it to 6.0 seconds, inspect 4.5 seconds, then advance it to 6.1. The first reading after the gap changes the smoothed estimate at 4.5 seconds while the filtered estimate there stays fixed. Both panels show horizontal position, and both bands are pointwise 95% intervals under the assumed model.

## What is the math?

The state is $\mathbf x_k=(p_x,p_y,v_x,v_y)^\top$: position and velocity in two dimensions. The constant-velocity model and position sensor are the same as in the filtering piece,

$$
\mathbf x_{k+1}=F\mathbf x_k+\mathbf w_k,
\qquad
\mathbf z_k=H\mathbf x_k+\mathbf e_k,
$$

where $F$ advances position by velocity times $\Delta t=0.1$ seconds, $H$ selects position, and the independent Gaussian noises have covariances $Q$ and $R$. The filter stores a mean $\mathbf m_k$ and covariance $P_k$ given measurements through $k$, together with the next prediction $\mathbf m^-_{k+1},P^-_{k+1}$. A missing measurement skips the correction step.

Smoothing asks for the same state given measurements through a later time $T$. The Rauch–Tung–Striebel recursion starts with $\mathbf m^s_T=\mathbf m_T$ and $P^s_T=P_T$, then works backwards (Rauch et al. 1965):

$$
G_k=P_kF^\top(P^-_{k+1})^{-1},
$$

$$
\mathbf m^s_k=\mathbf m_k+G_k(\mathbf m^s_{k+1}-\mathbf m^-_{k+1}),
$$

$$
P^s_k=P_k+G_k(P^s_{k+1}-P^-_{k+1})G_k^\top.
$$

The difference $\mathbf m^s_{k+1}-\mathbf m^-_{k+1}$ says how much the next state has been revised since the filter predicted it. The gain $G_k$ carries that revision back to the current state. At the last state there is no later evidence, so filtering and smoothing agree.

The gain comes from the same Gaussian conditioning identity used in the forward filter. Given measurements through $k$, adjacent states have cross-covariance $P_kF^\top$. Conditioning also on $\mathbf x_{k+1}$ gives the earlier state mean $\mathbf m_k+G_k(\mathbf x_{k+1}-\mathbf m^-_{k+1})$ and covariance $P_k-G_kP^-_{k+1}G_k^\top$. Once the next state is known, later measurements add no further information about the earlier one. Averaging over the smoothed next state gives the mean recursion; the law of total covariance gives the covariance recursion (Särkkä and Svensson 2023).

The plotted band is $m_{p_x}\pm1.96\sqrt{P_{p_xp_x}}$, using the filtered or smoothed covariance in the corresponding panel. It describes horizontal position at each time separately, rather than the probability that the whole path stays inside it.

## Why is it interesting?

The filter and smoother disagree because they answer questions with different information. An online tracker has to report where an object is using the frames received so far. After a video has been recorded, the rest of the frames are available too. A person emerging from behind a pillar tells us something about their route while they were hidden. Smoothing makes that later evidence part of the earlier estimate.

This is the same conditional structure as the [Brownian bridge](/pieces/brownian-bridge), where knowing an endpoint changes an entire path. Here the endpoint is noisy, velocity is hidden, and every later reading can contribute. The motion model supplies the connection across the gap. Without it, a position measured at 7 seconds would say nothing about position at 4.5 seconds.

The cutoff makes that connection visible. While it moves through the gap, no measurements are added and the smoothed estimate at a fixed earlier time stays unchanged. At 6.1 seconds the first new reading arrives, and a correction travels backwards through the missing interval. The filter's earlier estimates remain fixed because their information has not changed. A system that can tolerate a short delay can use the same idea over a moving window, trading latency for more evidence.

For a fixed linear Gaussian model, the smoothed covariance satisfies $P^s_k\preceq P_k$: uncertainty cannot increase in any direction of the state. The realised error need not fall at every time. The panel reports root-mean-square horizontal position error over the available record, including the gap, so the narrower band can be compared with what happened on this particular path. Lower $q$ to 0.05 and the distinction becomes sharper. The hidden path bends, but the model insists on nearly constant velocity; later data can then produce a narrow band around a poor reconstruction.

## How was it built?

The forward pass reuses the filtering piece's model, Joseph-form covariance update, and seeded sensor noise. The first reading initialises position with standard deviations $r$ and $2r$, while velocity starts at zero with standard deviation 2 along each axis. The process covariance is $q^2$ times $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\Delta t^3/3&\Delta t^2/2\\\Delta t^2/2&\Delta t\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ for each position-velocity pair, and $R=\operatorname{diag}(r^2,(2r)^2)$. The sliders change the assumed noise; the actual sensor standard deviations stay at 0.15 and 0.30.

The backward pass operates on the stored forward means and covariances. The axes are independent, so each gain needs only a two-by-two covariance block. Every seed generates the full sensor record before the gap and cutoff are applied. Moving the cutoff, switching the gap, or changing assumed noise therefore keeps the underlying readings fixed; **New noise sample** replaces them.

The SVG reveals both computed trajectories together in one left-to-right pass, then stops. The smoother already uses all readings through the selected cutoff; playback reveals the result rather than adding evidence. Moving a slider stops playback for inspection. The figure pauses off-screen and in hidden tabs, and visitors who prefer reduced motion can start it explicitly.

## Questions to try

1. Inspect 4.5 seconds and move the cutoff from 5.0 to 6.0, then to 6.1. Why does the smoothed estimate change only at the last step?
2. Set the inspected time equal to the cutoff. Why do filtering and smoothing agree there?
3. Switch the gap off. Does later evidence still change the earlier estimates?
4. Lower $q$ to 0.05 and draw several noise samples. Does a narrower band always mean a smaller error through the bend?

## Assumptions and limitations

The recursion is exact for the assumed linear Gaussian model, but the hidden sinusoidal path is not generated by that model. The bands can therefore miss the path more often than their nominal 95% suggests, especially when the assumed noise is too small. Noise parameters are set by hand, so their uncertainty is excluded. Only horizontal position is plotted, although both positions and velocities are estimated. Every reading belongs to the same object, with no clutter or data association. Smoothing also needs stored forward states and later measurements, which limits how quickly a revised estimate can be reported.
